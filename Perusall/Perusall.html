Algorithmic Injustice and Social Systems

In many contemporary systems, algorithms are deployed as seemingly neutral tools for ranking, sorting, and classifying people. Yet these systems are built on historical data that often reflects existing patterns of inequality. When such data is treated as objective fact, the resulting models can quietly reinforce the very injustices they were supposed to address.

Consider an algorithm that predicts which neighborhoods are most likely to generate calls for police service. If the model is trained on past call data without context, it will almost certainly mark the same over-policed communities as high-risk in the future. This does not necessarily mean that these communities are more dangerous; it may simply reflect that they have been watched more closely. The algorithm then directs additional attention back to those neighborhoods, deepening the cycle.

A more just approach to design would treat social context as a core requirement rather than an afterthought. Designers, researchers, and communities can work together to decide which outcomes matter, what counts as evidence, and how systems should respond when the data reflects harm. Without that work, algorithmic systems risk becoming polished interfaces for very old forms of discrimination.
